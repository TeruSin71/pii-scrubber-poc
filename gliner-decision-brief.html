PII Scrubber — GLiNER Result & the Decision It Puts in Front of You

Follow-up to Choosing the Detection Engine. The middle option between plain NER and a local LLM has now been built and measured. This brief reports what was found and frames the one decision that remains.

GLiNER FIGURES MEASURED · 16 AUG 2026 LOCAL LLM (OPTION A) STILL UNBUILT — ESTIMATES ONLY NOTHING NEW DEPLOYED — LIVE SYSTEM UNCHANGED

Where the previous brief left off. The deployed NER scrubber scores 90.0% on a fresh blind test, and its residual is almost entirely personal names in prose — the one class pattern rules were measured unable to reach. The open question was whether that residual justified a local LLM. Since then, a third engine was trialled: GLiNER, a small local language model that detects names by meaning rather than by memorized patterns — the "middle rung" between plain NER and a full LLM. It was built, run, and measured under the same discipline as everything else in this project: pre-registered expectations, blind to nothing it was graded on, nothing deployed.

What was found — the good half

Run in combination with the existing engine (each covers the other's blind spots), GLiNER closed every known miss on the project's internal test material — including every personal name: the all-caps surnames, the unusual names, the names in subject position that defeated every rule. It also caught the unnumbered street references ("the warehouse on Willis Street") that no pattern could. The two engines fail in almost perfectly different places: GLiNER reads names and misses structured values (IBANs, IPs, phone extensions); the deterministic rules do the exact opposite. Combined, nothing in the internal material leaked.

How to read that honestly. The internal test sets have been used throughout development, so "closed every known miss" means exactly that — the misses we knew about. It is not a product catch-rate. The honest final number would come from one fresh blind test, already prepared for and held in reserve, run once against the combined engine if it ships. Until then, 90.0% blind remains the only quotable figure, and it describes the current engine, not the combination.

Practicalities also measured: the combined engine fits the current free-tier infrastructure (2.2 GB of the 3 GB memory ceiling, under stress), and it is roughly 100× slower per request — fine for the document-upload path (65 documents in ~48 seconds, and that path is not time-critical), unknown for the live-incident path until measured on the real pod, with a fallback already agreed: if too slow, the live path keeps the current engine.

What was found — the cost half, and why it can't be engineered away

GLiNER is confidently wrong about ordinary business language. It reads "customer", "plant 4000", "warehouse team", "service desk" as people and organisations — often with higher confidence than it has on real names. Measured on 21 documents containing no PII at all:

EngineClean documents damaged by wrong redaction
Current engine (deployed today)2 of 21 (9.5%)
Combined engine, after all fixes7 of 21 (33%)
The sharpest example: a document reading "No customer or personal data in this one" came out as "No <PERSON> or personal data in this one" — the redaction inverting the sentence's meaning.

Every engineering remedy was tried or measured before this brief was written, under pre-registered tests: a smarter suppression rule was designed, proved safe, and shipped internally (it repaired one document and, as a side benefit, found and fixed a small coverage bug that had been in the deployed scrubber all along); confidence thresholds were measured and ruled out (GLiNER is confident when wrong); restricting GLiNER's categories was derived and ruled out (it relocates the damage rather than removing it); and a vocabulary campaign was measured to a ceiling of 4-of-21 — still double today's rate, with each added word carrying a real risk of hiding a genuine name. The floor is roughly 7 of 21. This is a property of the engine, not a bug awaiting a fix.

The shape of the decision

The two costs land on different paths, and the architecture already has an answer for one of them:

  • Live-incident path: over-redaction costs nothing here by design — identifiers are re-mapped inside our boundary, and triage matches the pattern and fix, not the customer. The only open question on this path is speed, measurable only on the real pod, with the fallback already agreed.
  • Upload path (the knowledge base): over-redaction damages readability — but this path has always had a human reviewer (BPA gate), and a mechanism already identified in the architecture — a restore step that lets the reviewer un-redact wrongly caught jargon with one action per token — turns the damage from permanent corpus noise into a few seconds of reviewer work per affected document.

So the real trade is human, not technical: the reviewer does a little more restoring on roughly a third of clean documents — work that is easy, visible, and impossible to get wrong silently — in exchange for the engine catching the personal names that are precisely what a tired reviewer is worst at spotting. A missed name slips through quietly; an over-redacted "plant 4000" is loud and fixable. Which direction of reviewer effort you prefer is the decision.

The three options now on the table

1 — Ship the combined engine, with the restore step

One release: combined engine + reviewer-restore in the BPA flow. Live-path speed measured at deployment, with the agreed fallback.

  • Closes the personal-name class — the entire known residual — on both paths (live path pending the speed gate).
  • Costs: build the restore step; more reviewer restore-work on the upload path; slower requests.
  • Finishes the project: one release, then the held blind test produces the final number.
  • Likely renders the local-LLM question moot at a fraction of its cost — pending the blind confirmation.

2 — Stay with the current engine

The deployed scrubber is stable, measured, and complete on its own terms.

  • Keeps 90.0% blind, clean corpus readability, fast responses, no new work.
  • The personal-name residual stands — caught by the human on the upload path, uncaught on the live path, where there is no human.
  • The local-LLM question returns in its original form: is that live residual acceptable, or does it justify Option A's cost?

3 — The local LLM (Option A) remains what it was in the previous brief: unbuilt, unmeasured here, higher cost and complexity. What has changed is its competitive position — the middle rung has now been measured catching every known name for a fraction of an LLM's cost and none of its operational burden. If option 1's blind test confirms the internal result, the case for Option A largely dissolves; if option 1 is declined, Option A is again the only route to the names.
